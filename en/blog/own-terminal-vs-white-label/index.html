Your own terminal or white-label: the economics

22 November 20266 min readWEBX GROUP

What each option actually buys

White-label is infrastructure rental: a working terminal — sometimes with liquidity, CRM and KYC — under your brand, for a subscription or licence. The code stays with the vendor. Building your own is an asset purchase: you pay for a project and receive the repository, infrastructure and documentation.

The difference is not “modern versus legacy”. It is what remains yours in three years:

  • Product. White-label: a configured vendor template. Own build: your product, your architecture.
  • Code. White-label: the vendor's. Own build: your repository.
  • Customisation. White-label: within platform limits. Own build: limited by budget and time.
  • Time to launch. White-label: weeks. Own build: two to four months to v1.
  • Dependency. White-label: vendor roadmap and tariffs. Own build: your own team.

Both models work. The mistake starts when the choice is made on “cheaper at launch” without modelling year three.

Public pricing, as of November 2026

Only published vendor tariffs below. Prices change — verify before running numbers.

  • B2Broker: B2CORE (CRM) from $1,000/mo, B2TRADER (trading platform) from $2,500/mo, B2COPY from $1,500/mo; subscriptions can be partly offset by liquidity commissions, up to $8,000/mo (b2broker.com/fees-and-pricing, checked 2026-11-22).
  • Leverate: trading platform from €1,690/mo (leverate.com/pricing/trading-platform, public price as of 2026-11-22; verify manually — the page was unreachable from our environment).
  • HollaEx: $35,000/yr + 15% revenue share or $69,000/yr with 0% rev share (Coinspeaker comparison, updated 2026-07-06).
  • OpenDAX: perpetual licence $50,000+ (same comparison, 2026-07-06).
  • PayBito: subscription $149–$12,000/mo depending on configuration (same comparison, 2026-07-06).

Our own published grid for reference: discovery from $2,500 (2–3 weeks), terminal from $25,000 (8–14 weeks), dedicated team $6,000–12,000/mo, support from $1,500/mo (pricing).

One clarification: white-label pricing excludes implementation, customisation, data migration and integrations with your providers. Vendors quote those separately — a budget risk of its own.

A 36-month TCO model with stated assumptions

Every TCO model is assumptions; these are explicit so you can recalculate. This is not a price promise and not a “who is cheaper” verdict — arithmetic on public tariffs.

A. Platform subscription (B2TRADER, $2,500/mo). 36 × $2,500 = $90,000 in subscriptions. Excluded: implementation, customisation, integrations, migration, liquidity commissions (which at B2Broker may offset subscriptions by up to $8,000/mo).

B. HollaEx licence. 3 × $35,000 + 15% rev share = $105,000 plus 15% of a turnover you cannot forecast. The $69,000/yr, 0% rev share variant: $207,000 over 36 months, without revenue share.

C. Own build (our public reference point). Terminal from $25,000 + support from $1,500/mo × 33 months ≈ $74,500. Excluded: work beyond MVP scope and infrastructure (servers, market data, provider licences).

The scenarios sit in the same order of magnitude. The difference is not “own build is cheaper” — it is where money goes and what you hold afterwards. In A and B you still do not own the code after three years: ending the subscription means stopping or migrating. In C you own a product to develop, white-label or show in due diligence.

And the 15% rev share grows with turnover: it is the only line that scales against you, and the one usually omitted from the “subscription is cheaper” comparison.

When white-label is genuinely better

  • You need a licensed brokerage stack. Liquidity, provider bridges, regulator reporting. Rebuilding it is pointless unless you are an infrastructure company.
  • Launch is measured in weeks. A market window, a partnership, a regulatory deadline. A subscription plus a template ships faster than any project.
  • The product is not the core business. If you earn on commissions and client base and the terminal is a utility, custom code is early.
  • You have no engineering team. A custom product needs operations, not just development.

If two of these describe you, white-label or a hybrid is rational — not a compromise.

When to build your own, and how to avoid lock-in

Own build pays off when the product is the business, and at least two hold: the platform cannot deliver the screens, data or flows you need; your data and integrations cannot live inside someone else's architecture; you plan to sell the platform; investor due diligence is ahead; you need predictable cost instead of a growing subscription and revenue share.

Rules that reduce lock-in risk either way:

  1. Code ownership in the contract. Who owns it, what happens on termination, whether the repository transfers.
  2. Data export. The ability to pull history, trades and clients without the vendor's involvement.
  3. Explicit exit terms. Notice period, termination cost, the fate of customisations.
  4. Your own infrastructure for sensitive data. If data cannot leave, know it before signing.
  5. Documented architecture. Even with white-label, keep integration docs — they save months on migration.

How it works in practice is in the N9 case: we build a SaaS terminal in iterations, starting with the data pipeline rather than the interface. To model TCO for your scenario, book a 30-minute diagnostic call; scope is on the trading terminals page.

FAQ

Can we start on white-label and move to our own terminal later?

Yes, and often that is the sensible path: validate demand on ready infrastructure, then build. Fix exit terms, export rights and the fate of customisations upfront — otherwise migration costs more than the development would have.

What does it cost to support a custom terminal?

It depends on scope: data pipeline, integrations, frontend and infrastructure need different attention. In our grid, post-warranty support starts at $1,500/mo, a full dedicated team is $6,000–12,000/mo (pricing). Compare it with a vendor subscription together with scope, not as a single number.

White-label looks cheaper at the start. Why not always choose it?

Because only the subscription is visible at the start. Implementation, quoted-on-request customisation, a revenue share that grows with turnover, and exit costs are not. If the product is the core of your business, three years of subscription can cost the same as an own build — and you still will not own the code.
