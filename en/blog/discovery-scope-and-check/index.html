Discovery in three weeks: how to scope a fintech MVP before any code

21 November 20266 min readWEBX GROUP

What you are actually buying

“How much will the MVP cost?” cannot be answered before the scope exists. “Let's start and see” suits the vendor, not a founder whose runway ends in a quarter.

Discovery buys certainty for a fixed price and duration. In our pricing it is a package from $2,500 over 2–3 weeks with a fixed scope to match: interviews, architecture, data schema, roadmap, estimate and a prototype of 5–8 key screens.

Two questions get confused here: what to build (scenarios, MVP boundaries, ordering) and what it costs (sprint ranges, assumptions, risks). A deck that answers only the first is marketing; a number that answers only the second is a guess nobody trusts.

The process itself is covered in “Discovery in three weeks”; this article is about commissioning and accepting it.

How the work runs: brief and artefacts by week

Demand artefacts week by week rather than at the end: then you can tell by day five whether work is happening.

The brief

Without it, three weeks are spent discovering what the founder could have explained in an hour. Minimum input: who the user is and which decision the product supports; where money flows and when; which integrations are mandatory at launch (payment provider, KYC, exchange, bank); what already exists; the external deadline and what must be ready for it; which data is personal; who decides on product and who approves budget; what a failed first version looks like.

The last item is the one everyone skips: without a stated failure condition, every feature looks mandatory and the scope grows exactly when it should be locked.

Week 1 — scenarios and constraints

Output: a scenario map (who does what, what happens to the money), integrations with risks, constraints (licences, data, deadlines). A good sign is an explicit money-flow diagram: funds in, where they are accounted for, funds out, who is legally responsible.

Week 2 — architecture and data

Output: C4-level architecture (context, containers, key flows), a stack decision with rationale, data schema and event model, wireframes and a UI concept for key screens. For an MVP the right answer is usually a modular monolith with explicit module boundaries, not a service fleet built for a future that may never arrive.

Week 3 — roadmap and estimate

Output: a 3–6 month roadmap split into two-week sprints, an estimate in person-day ranges with assumptions and an explicit exclusions list, plus the screen prototype. If week three produces a deck without an estimate, you bought a pre-sales pitch.

How to audit the estimate

Estimates are the most forged artefact in software. Four checks:

Ranges, not points. “74 days” is a bad estimate. “6–9 weeks with two engineers and one QA, assuming two integrations rather than five” is a good one. Precision is inversely proportional to honesty.

Assumptions written down. Ask: what has to be true for this timeline to hold? The answer should fill half a page.

Sensitivity. Recalculate under three changes: twice the integrations, one engineer fewer, scope halved. If the number does not move, it was not built from work items.

Scenarios, not features. “An account screen — two days” sounds fine until the KYC integration, webhook handling and status reconciliation take two weeks. Correct estimates attach to flows, not to a list of screens.

Acceptance and red flags

  1. In-scope and out-of-scope for v1 are written down.
  2. Mandatory integrations are listed with a risk per integration.
  3. Architecture and data schema exist, not just prose about how great it will be.
  4. The estimate is in ranges with assumptions and exclusions.
  5. The build order under a halved budget is clear.
  6. Success criteria are a metric, not “more money”.
  7. A prototype of key screens exists and has been discussed.
  8. There is a plan B for “the integration does not work”.

Afterwards you should answer five questions in your own words: what ships in v1; which integrations are mandatory; how long and how much, given assumptions; what to build first if the budget is cut; how you will know the product works. If the answer is “more research is needed”, the questions were wrong.

Red flags: discovery without artefacts (forty slides, no architecture.md); an estimate without assumptions; “free discovery” that is a pre-sales deck or work priced into the project later; discovery stretched to two months, which is a scope problem; development starting before the scope is locked.

What happens afterwards

Discovery ends with a decision, not a document. Three outcomes:

  1. Build with this studio. The estimate becomes a sprint plan; assumptions become what you verify at the first demo.
  2. Build with another team. The artefacts are yours — confirm upfront that source documents and design files transfer.
  3. Do not build. Sometimes the product is not needed or the economics do not work. That is the cheapest way to learn bad news — a success, not a failure.

Packages are on the pricing page. With an idea or a rough prototype, start with a 30-minute diagnostic call. If a project is already under way, go to an audit; if you need a team after discovery, see the dedicated team page.

FAQ

Can discovery be done faster than three weeks?

Yes, if scenarios, integrations and data already exist. But speed costs depth: without interviews and a money-flow diagram the estimate becomes a guess.

The scope and price are fixed — what if we need more?

Anything beyond the fixed scope becomes a separate stage. In practice people extend integration research or add screens — before development starts, not instead of it.

How is discovery different from an audit?

Discovery is about a product that does not exist yet: scenarios, architecture, estimate. An audit is about code that already exists: what is broken, what can stay, what it costs to ship. They are often done in sequence.
