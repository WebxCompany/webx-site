Discovery in three weeks: scoping a fintech MVP

18 November 20265 min readWEBX GROUP

“How much will the MVP cost?” is a question that cannot be answered honestly without a scope. “Let's start and see” only suits the vendor. Discovery is how you buy certainty for a fixed price and three weeks instead of guessing from a chat description.

Below is how we run discovery for fintech products — from interviews to an estimate you can show an investor.

Why discovery and why a fixed price

Discovery has a concrete result: you know what to build, what it costs and in what order. The price is fixed, as is the scope. It is not open-ended consulting but a package of artefacts: architecture, roadmap, estimate and a prototype of key screens.

Fintech differs from ordinary web work because architecture mistakes cost more: provider integrations, licences, personal data, balance reconciliation. Changing the money-flow model in sprint five is not a refactor — it is restarting part of the product.

Week 1: scenarios and constraints

We start with people and money, not screens. Who the user is, which decisions they make, what happens to their money at every step. For a fintech product we draw the money-flow diagram: where funds come from, where they are accounted for, where they leave, and who is legally responsible.

The first week's questions sound boring but define the budget:

  • Which integrations are mandatory at launch: payment provider, KYC, exchange, bank?
  • Where does our code end and the provider's licensed infrastructure begin?
  • Which data is personal and what is needed to store it?
  • Is there an external deadline — investor, launch, regulator — and what must be ready for it?

Week 2: architecture and data

Once scenarios are clear we draw C4-level architecture: context, containers, key data flows. An MVP does not need a microservice zoo — a modular monolith on Next.js and Node/Go with one database and clear module boundaries is often enough. Complexity is added only where it pays off: realtime, integrations, background jobs.

We design the data schema and event model separately: idempotency, webhook handling, payment statuses, logging. This is where requirements that save weeks later appear: “what if a webhook arrives twice” and “how do we know a payment is stuck”. In parallel we produce wireframes and a UI concept for 5–8 key screens — to verify that scenarios converge into an interface.

Week 3: roadmap and estimate

An estimate without sprint breakdown is a number nobody believes. So we split the work into two-week sprints: what is in the MVP, what moves to the next version, what is not needed at launch. Each item gets a person-day range, not an exact figure — precision in estimation is a sign of a bad estimate.

The output is three documents: architecture.md with decisions and risks, roadmap.md for 3–6 months with priorities, estimate.xlsx with ranges, assumptions and an explicit list of exclusions — plus a prototype of key screens.

Definition of done for discovery

Discovery is finished not when three weeks pass but when its artefacts answer five questions:

  1. What exactly ships in v1 and what is out of scope?
  2. Which integrations are mandatory and what risks come with them?
  3. How much time and money will the MVP take, given the assumptions?
  4. In what order to build if the budget is halved?
  5. How will we know the product works — beyond “more money”?

Four typical mistakes

First: estimating features instead of scenarios. “An account screen — two days” sounds reasonable until integrations and error states, which take longer than the layout, are counted. Second: forgetting integration cost. In fintech each provider connection is a contract, a sandbox, webhook handling, reconciliation and monitoring. Third: starting with design. Pretty screens without a data model look convincing right up to the first conversation with engineering. Fourth: not fixing success criteria. If it is unclear which metric means the product works, every feature seems mandatory.

Discovery is the cheapest part of a project. A mistake here costs days; in development it costs sprints; in production it costs money and reputation. If you want your task reviewed, start with an audit or a diagnostic call; package prices are on the pricing page.
